# 02 — Competitive Landscape (Brief §41, §42)

## A. The Kenyan graveyard — read this first

### Lynk (2015–2022)

**[FACT]** Founded in Nairobi by Adam Grunewald and Johannes Degn. Connected households and businesses with **verified domestic workers, fundis, artisans and blue-collar professionals**, across dozens of categories including furniture, beauty, installation, repair and maintenance. Acquired by Eden Life (Nigeria) in 2022, facilitated by Enza Capital.

**[FACT] What they learned, in their own framing:**
- The defining early question was **lead-gen vs full-service** — connect and step away, or own the whole delivery.
- They **pivoted operating models multiple times** searching for one that worked at scale in Nairobi.
- They shifted **from an auction marketplace to a standardised service model**.
- **Vetting and training costs were high.** Partnerships with vocational training providers never took off, forcing them to build **Lynk Academy** in-house for recruiting, vetting and training.
- **Class friction was a real product problem:** customers, often from significantly higher social classes than the providers, would unfairly mistrust tradespeople — assuming they were cheating or delivering poor work — because of prior bad experience or communication difficulties.

**[REC] Three lessons to internalise:**
1. **The auction/bidding model failed and they moved to standardisation.** Your brief's section 8 "Post a Task → providers quote" is the model Lynk abandoned. Use it narrowly, not as the default.
2. **Supply vetting is the cost centre that breaks the model.** Any plan that assumes cheap provider onboarding is wrong. Budget for it or pick a vertical where supply is easier to verify.
3. **[ASSUMPTION]** The class-mistrust finding suggests the platform must arbitrate *perception*, not just quality — structured scope, photographic evidence, fixed pricing and a neutral dispute process exist partly to protect providers from unfair customer judgement. That is a product requirement, not a nicety.

### SweepSouth in Kenya (2019–2022)

**[FACT]** Founded 2014 by Aisha Pandor and Alen Ribic; **$15M+ raised**; ~40,000 bookings/month across its markets. Entered **Kenya 2019**, Nigeria June 2022, **closed both on 25 November 2022**, citing economic conditions. Commentary described the expansions as half-hearted, with shoddy operational setup, slow technology and unstable websites. Still operating in South Africa, and in Egypt via its FilKhedma acquisition.

**[REC] The lesson is not "cleaning doesn't work" — it works in South Africa.** The lesson is that **home-services marketplaces do not travel**, because the asset is local operational capability, not software. This cuts both ways for you: it is why an outside player is unlikely to crush you in Nairobi, and why your own expansion to Kampala will be a fresh build rather than a rollout.

### Who is operating in Kenya now

**[FACT]** My-Fundi (verified plumbers, electricians, carpenters), Balozy (fundi marketplace spanning plumbers, DJs, tutors; multi-city — Nairobi, Kisumu, Nyeri, Eldoret, Bomet, Siaya), Kalinoi (cleaning, caregiving, gardening, events, tech repairs **and social-content creators**), Juakali Hub, Cleaner-Kenya, plus classifieds like PigiaMe.

**[REC] Note Kalinoi carefully — it already spans home services *and* creators.** The horizontal concept in your brief is not unclaimed. **[ASSUMPTION]** I could not verify any of these players' scale, funding or transaction volume; their public presence suggests early stage. **Sign up for each and transact before finalising strategy** — desk research cannot tell you whether these are real marketplaces or directories with a booking form.

## B. Africa beyond Kenya

| Company | Market | **[FACT]** Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Kandua** | South Africa | Raised **$13M**; **acquired by Santam (insurer), May 2024**; had built embedded financial services; launching an AI "home companion" in 2026 |
| **SweepSouth** | SA, Egypt | $15M+; exited Kenya and Nigeria 2022; acquired FilKhedma (Egypt) |
| **Eden Life** | Nigeria, Kenya | Raised **$2.26M**; subscription home services; acquired Lynk 2022; **has since rebranded toward an e-commerce marketplace** |

**[REC] Two signals worth more than the rest of this document:**

1. **Kandua's exit was to an insurer, not a tech acquirer.** The value that accumulated was a verified network of vetted tradespeople and a claim on the home-services relationship — an *insurance distribution and risk* asset. This is the honest version of the "ledger as moat" thesis, and it is a plausible outcome, not a plan.
2. **Eden Life bought a services marketplace and then drifted to e-commerce.** **[ASSUMPTION]** That is the behaviour of a company that found selling *goods* easier to scale than selling *labour*. Treat it as a caution.

## C. Global models — what actually worked

| Platform | Model | **[FACT]** Outcome / economics | Transferable lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Urban Company** | **Managed vertical marketplace** — controls onboarding, training, equipment, pricing, scheduling, QC | 50,000+ professionals; provider incomes reportedly 3–5×; India home-services TAM ₹4,990–5,070B (2024) → ₹8,220–8,350B (2029) | **The only unambiguous success, and it is managed and vertical.** "Pure marketplace aggregation didn't work — supply quality couldn't be controlled." |
| **TaskRabbit** | Horizontal task marketplace | Acquired by IKEA 2017; LTV:CAC ~**4:1** | Retention is achievable, but it needed a strategic owner |
| **Handy** | Home services | Acquired by ANGI; **struggled to exceed 2:1 LTV:CAC** | Churn kills home services |
| **Thumbtack** | **Lead generation** — pros pay per lead | **$400M revenue FY2024 (+27%)**; **$8–150+ per lead, shared with 4–5 pros** | Big revenue, but revenue decouples from completed jobs |
| **Airtasker** | Transactional marketplace | **AUD 52.7M revenue FY2025**; ~10–20% service fee; 92% transaction-based | A real but modest business after a decade |
| Lead-gen marketplaces generally | — | **Repeat rates as low as 15%** | Lead-gen does not build habit |

### The three structural findings

**1. Managed beats marketplace in physical services; marketplace beats managed in digital services.**
Physical work has variable quality that only training and control can fix. Digital work has portfolios you can inspect before buying. **[REC] This is why your creator vertical can stay light-touch while your fundi vertical cannot** — and why treating them as one product is a mistake.

**2. Lead-gen monetises well and serves customers badly.**
**[FACT]** Thumbtack does $400M on a model where a pro pays $8–150 for a lead shared with 4–5 competitors, whether or not it converts. **[REC] Do not build this in Kenya.** It extracts from providers who cannot afford it, produces no completion data, and generates the reputation-free experience your entire thesis is against. It is also the fastest way to be resented by supply.

**3. The transactional model is the right one and it is slower.**
Airtasker's ~10–20% on completed work is the honest version. It grows more slowly than lead-gen and it compounds, because every transaction produces trust data.

## D. What none of them solved, and you might

**[REC]** Three gaps are genuinely open, and two of them are specific to your market:

1. **The remote principal.** No platform above serves *"I am not there; act for me and tell me the truth."* TaskRabbit assumes you are home. Urban Company assumes you are home. **[FACT]** Kenya has US$5B/year in remittances and documented, worsening fraud against distant buyers. This is the wedge in `00 §5`.
2. **Materials money.** Kenyan fundi work routinely involves the customer advancing cash for materials, which is where trust most often breaks. No platform I found treats the materials advance as a distinct escrowed, receipted, evidenced stage. **[REC] This is a concrete, buildable differentiator for the fundi vertical.**
3. **Provider-side dignity and professionalisation.** **[FACT]** Urban Company's stated moat is value creation on the supply side — training providers and multiplying their incomes. Lynk found the same and had to build an academy. **[REC] In a market where supply is abundant and unverified, the platform that makes providers *more employable* owns supply.**
